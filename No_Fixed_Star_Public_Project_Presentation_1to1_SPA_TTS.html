The Project at a Glance

No Fixed Star is a spacefaring RPG and simulation built around a simple promise: your ship is not a menu, your crew are not passive bonuses, and the galaxy does not wait politely for you to arrive.

The player begins with a modest working vessel and a small crew. Jobs create movement. Movement consumes resources. Travel creates risk. Damage creates mechanical problems. Mechanical problems create economic and social choices. Those choices shape reputation, relationships, access, and the next job.

What This Is Not

•
Not a conventional arcade dogfighter built around endless combat.
•
Not a spreadsheet economy detached from physical places.
•
Not a base-building empire game disguised as a ship RPG.
•
Not an unrestricted first-person shooter.
•
Not a progression system where numbers replace physical changes to the ship.
•
Not a universe where every part, service, and commodity can be summoned remotely from a universal store.
PROJECT AREA 01

Project Vision & Player Experience

The entire project is organized around connected causes and consequences.
•
A working ship serves as home, workplace, vehicle, and persistent character.
•
The player builds capability through equipment, crew expertise, contacts, tools, knowledge, and access.
•
The intended rhythm alternates between preparation, travel, work, crisis, recovery, and new opportunity.
PROJECT AREA 02

The StingWasp: The Player's First Home

The starter ship establishes the scale and intimacy of the experience.
•
A compact two-deck working transport supports freight, crew life, maintenance, engineering, navigation, and emergency response.
•
Exterior, cutaway, room, system, and component views all refer to the same persistent vessel.
•
Wear, repairs, modifications, cargo, damage, and personal history make each player's ship increasingly distinct.
WHAT THE PLAYER FEELS This is not a disposable starter vehicle. It is the first place the player learns, damages, repairs, modifies, and eventually knows by heart.
PROJECT AREA 03

Ship Interior & Habitable Spaces

Rooms are functional spaces rather than decorative corridors.
•
Bridge and navigation spaces support command and situational awareness.
•
Engineering and service areas expose the machinery that keeps the vessel alive.
•
Crew quarters, mess, medical, storage, cargo, utility, and emergency spaces support daily life.
•
Doors and compartments matter during fire, decompression, contamination, boarding, and repair.
PROJECT AREA 04

Power & Electrical Systems

Electrical capacity creates understandable operational choices.
•
Ship systems compete for finite power during damage or high demand.
•
Emergency power and prioritization allow the player to preserve critical functions.
•
Electrical faults can turn a mechanical problem into a ship-wide crisis.
•
Crew and player decisions determine which systems stay alive when capacity is limited.
PROJECT AREA 05

Cooling, Heat & Thermal Stress

Performance has physical consequences.
•
Engines, power systems, weapons, and other machinery generate heat.
•
Cooling failures can reduce performance or force shutdowns.
•
Emergency overdrive may save the ship while creating later maintenance problems.
•
Thermal stress connects tactical decisions to long-term wear and repair.
PROJECT AREA 06

Life Support & Habitability

Keeping the crew alive is part of operating the ship.
•
Atmosphere, oxygen, temperature, contamination, smoke, and emergency reserves affect occupied spaces.
•
Damage to life-support equipment changes which compartments remain safe.
•
Long journeys consume supplies and make preparation meaningful.
•
Emergency isolation and repair can preserve part of the ship even when another section becomes hazardous.
PROJECT AREA 07

Propulsion, Fuel & Navigation

Travel is a decision, not a loading screen with decorative stars.
•
Local flight supports tactical positioning and approach to destinations.
•
Longer travel considers time, fuel, ship condition, route knowledge, and risk.
•
Ship condition affects mobility and operational confidence.
•
Travel can be accelerated when safe and interrupted when events demand attention.
PROJECT AREA 08

Sensors, Detection & Information

Knowledge is a resource.
•
Contacts progress from unknown signals to identified ships or hazards.
•
Better sensors improve detection, identification, navigation, and tactical awareness.
•
Active scanning can reveal more information while increasing exposure.
•
Market and route knowledge can become stale, making fresh intelligence valuable.
PROJECT AREA 09

Tactical Space Encounters

Combat is about decisions, systems, position, and survival.
•
Weapons, sensors, maneuvering, damage, heat, and crew readiness interact.
•
Subsystem damage can matter more than simply reducing a hull bar.
•
Different opponents use different priorities and behaviors.
•
Retreat, evasion, negotiation, surrender, or escape can be valid outcomes when circumstances turn ugly.
WHAT THE PLAYER FEELS Combat remains tense because survival may mean protecting a crippled system, buying time for the crew, or escaping with enough ship left to reach port.
PROJECT AREA 10

Damage, Fire & Decompression

A hit can become a story that lasts well beyond the battle.
•
Damage can affect hull sections, rooms, machinery, atmosphere, and crew access.
•
Fire and smoke can make routes unsafe and force emergency responses.
•
A damaged system may continue operating poorly, fail completely, or require a temporary workaround.
•
Damage persists until the player actually deals with it.
PROJECT AREA 11

Component Inspection & Maintenance

The player can get close enough to understand what is wrong.
•
Important machinery is represented as physical assemblies and replaceable components.
•
Inspection reveals condition, faults, connections, and maintenance needs.
•
Repairs can involve safe shutdown, access, removal, testing, replacement, reinstallation, and calibration.
•
The depth is focused on meaningful machinery rather than turning every screw into a minigame.
WHAT THE PLAYER FEELS Maintenance turns technical trouble into meaningful choices about time, money, risk, tools, facilities, and who aboard can actually do the work.
PROJECT AREA 12

Repair, Rebuild & Improvisation

Keeping an old ship flying is a major form of progression.
•
Field repairs stabilize problems when proper facilities are unavailable.
•
Shipboard maintenance handles routine work and component replacement.
•
Specialized facilities are required for increasingly complex repairs.
•
Jury-rigs can restore limited function at a cost, creating stories the player may carry for a long time.
PROJECT AREA 13

Cargo & Physical Logistics

Freight is something the ship actually carries.
•
Cargo consumes capacity and has ownership, condition, destination, and sometimes environmental requirements.
•
Mission cargo remains distinct from ordinary inventory.
•
Hazardous, refrigerated, live, restricted, or fragile freight creates operational complications.
•
Transfer, storage, concealment, inspection, and loss all have consequences.
PROJECT AREA 14

Crew Skills, Needs & Health

Crew members are people with capability and limits.
•
Skills influence repair, navigation, medical work, engineering, security, and other tasks.
•
Fatigue, injury, safety, food, pay, morale, and working conditions can affect performance.
•
Duty assignments and equipment shape what each crew member can accomplish.
•
A brilliant specialist who is exhausted, injured, or furious is still a problem. Humanity remains inconvenient.
PROJECT AREA 15

Crew Autonomy & Daily Work

The ship should feel inhabited even when the player is not issuing every movement.
•
Crew travel through real ship spaces to reach stations, tools, supplies, and jobs.
•
Routine work can be assigned and prioritized.
•
Emergency conditions interrupt normal schedules.
•
Hazards can alter routes and make inaccessible spaces operationally significant.
PROJECT AREA 16

Crew Relationships & Personal Stories

Shared work and danger create relationships.
•
Trust, conflict, loyalty, ethics, pay, risk, and shared history influence crew dynamics.
•
Routine spaces such as the mess, bunks, bridge, workshop, and medical area support character moments.
•
Personal problems and loyalties can create opportunities, complications, or departures.
•
Crew stories emerge from both authored material and the situations created by play.
WHAT THE PLAYER FEELS The player sees the ship at the scale appropriate to the current problem, from a vessel in space to a room, system, and physical component.
PROJECT AREA 17

Camera & Presentation

The player can move smoothly between strategic and intimate scales.
•
Exterior views present the ship in space.
•
Cutaway views reveal rooms, crew, and emergencies.
•
System overlays explain power, atmosphere, cooling, and other ship states.
•
Close inspection supports machinery and component work.
•
System and galaxy maps expand the view without turning the ship into an irrelevant icon.
PROJECT AREA 18

Interface & Alerts

Complex systems should create decisions, not confusion.
•
Persistent status information summarizes ship, crew, location, time, and critical alerts.
•
Contextual inspection explains the currently selected room, system, component, cargo, crew member, or contact.
•
Unavailable actions explain what is missing rather than merely refusing.
•
Alert severity helps the player distinguish routine issues from genuine emergencies.
PROJECT AREA 19

Local Space & Traffic

The galaxy has other people moving through it.
•
Civilian, industrial, security, pirate, and mission traffic create local activity.
•
Nearby entities receive detailed behavior while distant activity can continue at a broader level.
•
Encounters can emerge naturally from travel, commerce, faction activity, and world events.
•
Stations, ships, wrecks, hazards, and approach lanes make local space feel purposeful.
PROJECT AREA 20

Docking, Landing & Arrival

Destinations are places where the current journey resolves into new choices.
•
Docking connects the ship to markets, repairs, contracts, crew, customs, storage, and social opportunities.
•
Planetary and asteroid destinations use contained arrival and landing experiences.
•
The ship remains the player's anchor when visiting other places.
•
Departure requires the player to live with the decisions made while in port.
PROJECT AREA 21

System & Galaxy Navigation

The maps are planning tools, not omniscient catalogues.
•
Routes show known destinations, hazards, faction influence, travel cost, and available intelligence.
•
Information can be incomplete or outdated.
•
The player plans around ship range, fuel, risk, deadlines, and known services.
•
Maps can reveal leads and known suppliers without becoming a magical remote shopping interface.
WHAT THE PLAYER FEELS A shortage is not merely a red number. It can be the consequence of a lost shipment, blockade, disaster, raid, strike, or sudden demand.
PROJECT AREA 22

Markets & Regional Economy

Prices and shortages have reasons.
•
Locations produce, consume, import, and export goods.
•
Shipments take time and can be delayed, diverted, seized, or destroyed.
•
Shortages can change prices, repair availability, contract demand, and local conditions.
•
The player's actions can participate in these chains without requiring them to manage an interstellar corporation.
PROJECT AREA 23

Trade, Vendors & Services

Commerce is local and contextual.
•
New, used, rebuilt, and salvaged equipment can differ in condition, history, and value.
•
Barter, favors, contacts, and reputation can matter alongside credits.
•
Restricted goods and services depend on local law and access.
•
Repair quotes reflect the work, capability, parts, and time involved.
WHAT THE PLAYER FEELS Jobs connect the systems. A delivery can become a combat problem, then a repair problem, then a market problem, then a relationship.
PROJECT AREA 24

Contracts & Jobs

Jobs are the engine that keeps the ship moving.
•
Freight, courier, passenger, salvage, escort, recovery, survey, bounty, smuggling, repair, and relief work support different play styles.
•
Jobs can emerge from shortages, danger, politics, breakdowns, lost shipments, and local needs.
•
Deadlines and obligations interact with travel time and ship condition.
•
Outcomes affect reputation, reliability, relationships, and future access.
PROJECT AREA 25

Factions, Law & Jurisdiction

Crossing a border can change what is legal, safe, or valuable.
•
Factions have different interests, relationships, security practices, and commercial rules.
•
Territory affects inspections, patrols, restricted equipment, contracts, and consequences.
•
Faction actions continue beyond the player's immediate location.
•
The player can become trusted, tolerated, watched, disliked, or wanted in different places.
PROJECT AREA 26

Stations, Shipyards & Settlements

Locations are differentiated by what they can actually provide.
•
Markets, repair capability, shipyards, recruitment, storage, customs, black markets, and contracts vary by destination.
•
Not every port can repair every system.
•
Industrial, frontier, civilian, security, salvage, and corporate locations create distinct opportunities.
•
Local events and shortages can change what a familiar destination offers.
PROJECT AREA 27

Away Operations & Boarding

Sometimes the job requires leaving the ship.
•
Contained tactical spaces support derelicts, boarding actions, recovery sites, facilities, and other focused missions.
•
The player chooses who leaves the ship, temporarily changing onboard capability.
•
Hazards, doors, navigation, equipment, injuries, and recovered items persist back into ship play.
•
The feature remains deliberately contained so the project stays a ship-and-crew RPG rather than wandering off to become an unrelated shooter.
PROJECT AREA 28

Salvage, Wrecks & Smuggling

The margins of civilization create opportunity.
•
Wrecks can contain identifiable machinery, cargo, evidence, and recoverable components.
•
Salvage may have ownership or legal consequences.
•
Recovered parts retain condition and history.
•
Concealed cargo, manifests, inspections, and local law create risk for players who choose illicit work.
PROJECT AREA 29

Progression & Ship Evolution

Progression changes what the player can physically do.
•
Better components alter real ship capability.
•
Better tools and crew expertise open new maintenance and operational options.
•
Contacts and reputation provide access to places, information, equipment, and work.
•
The ship's configuration becomes a record of the player's priorities and history.
PROJECT AREA 30

World Content & Variety

The systemic foundation supports a broad but coherent galaxy.
•
Manufacturers, factions, industries, settlements, wrecks, ships, stations, and equipment families create recognizable regional identity.
•
Reusable modular content supports scale while hero locations and distinctive assets prevent procedural sameness.
•
New content is designed to interact with the same core rules rather than becoming isolated feature islands.
•
The goal is variety that remains mechanically meaningful.
PROJECT AREA 31

3D Visual Asset Direction

The physicality of the project depends on readable, production-quality 3D assets.
•
Ships and major equipment use consistent real-world scale.
•
Important machinery is designed to support exterior, cutaway, system, and inspection views.
•
Materials, wear, industrial construction, modularity, and recognizable silhouettes reinforce the working-spacecraft identity.
•
Asset detail scales appropriately between hero objects, interactive machinery, environment pieces, and background content.
PROJECT AREA 32

Audio & Music

The ship should be understandable with the ears as well as the eyes.
•
Machinery ambience changes as systems degrade or fail.
•
Room conditions, pressure, power, alarms, impacts, and combat influence the soundscape.
•
Critical alerts take priority over routine ambience.
•
Music supports travel, tension, combat, recovery, and the lonely rhythm of life between jobs.
WHAT THE PLAYER FEELS The vertical slice demonstrates the project's defining promise: one event can propagate through ship systems, crew behavior, logistics, markets, and the next adventure.
PROJECT AREA 33

Accessibility & Player Control

Systemic depth should not require unnecessary physical or sensory barriers.
•
Input remapping, scalable interface presentation, and multiple alert channels are part of the core design.
•
Important information is not communicated by color alone.
•
Motion and time-control options help players manage dense situations.
•
Automation can reduce repetitive management while preserving the same underlying rules and consequences.
PROJECT AREA 34

Performance & Scale

The universe can feel large without pretending every distant bolt needs a full simulation.
•
Nearby ships, crew, hazards, and machinery receive the highest detail.
•
Distant activity continues at an appropriate strategic level.
•
Visual detail scales with distance and importance.
•
The player should experience continuity of cause and effect without paying for invisible complexity every frame.
PROJECT AREA 35

The Vertical Slice

One integrated scenario proves the entire identity of No Fixed Star.
•
The player accepts temperature-sensitive medical freight and physically carries it aboard.
•
Travel leads to a hostile encounter that damages sensors, cooling, and a compartment.
•
Crew respond to the emergency while the player stabilizes the ship and escapes.
•
At the destination, a missing replacement part cannot simply be bought because local supply has been disrupted.
•
The player follows leads, barters or salvages, acquires a compatible part, repairs the ship, and continues onward.
PROJECT AREA 36

Full Game Production

Once the connected experience works, the galaxy expands through content rather than incompatible new rules.
•
Additional ship classes, manufacturers, regions, factions, equipment, jobs, locations, characters, and events broaden the experience.
•
Frontier, industrial, corporate, civilian, salvage, security, and rare high-end content provide distinct environments and economies.
•
New content reinforces the core cycle of crew, work, travel, consequence, repair, and opportunity.
•
The final game is intended to feel broad because systems combine, not because every possible genre has been stapled onto it.
PROJECT AREA 37

The Player's Long-Term Story

The campaign is the history of what happened to the ship and the people aboard it.
•
The player may become a respected carrier, fixer, salvager, smuggler, specialist, survivor, or something less easily summarized.
•
Crew relationships and departures create a personal history.
•
Repairs, modifications, salvaged parts, old damage, and improvised fixes create a mechanical history.
•
Faction standing, debts, favors, contacts, and past jobs create a social history.
•
There is no fixed star telling the player who they must become.

No Fixed Star

No Fixed Star is built around the idea that a small working ship can support an enormous range of stories when its machinery, crew, cargo, jobs, markets, destinations, and consequences belong to the same world.

A broken sensor can become a maintenance problem. The missing part can become a travel problem. Travel can become a risk. A shortage can become a market condition. That condition can create a job. The job can change a relationship. The relationship can change what happens the next time the ship leaves port.

The objective is not simply to give the player a galaxy to visit. It is to make the distance between places matter, to make the people aboard matter, and to make the ship that carries them feel worth keeping alive.
